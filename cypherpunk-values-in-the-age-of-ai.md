# Cypherpunk Values in the Age of AI

I am a cypherpunk at heart.

Privacy, censorship resistance, permissionlessness, and unstoppability protect people against power. Systems that require permission can exclude dissidents. Payment networks that freeze money can become tools of coercion. Private communication protects our ability to think, organize, and resist.

Criminals use these protections too. I have accepted that tradeoff because giving authorities control over everyone’s lives seems more dangerous.

AI makes me question whether that balance will hold.

## What AI changes

AI lowers the barriers to harmful action. It makes specialist knowledge easier to use and automates work that once needed skilled people. A person’s ability to cause harm no longer depends as much on their own expertise or on finding people willing to help.

Some harms spread beyond the initial act. A contagious disease, for example, crosses borders without further effort from whoever started it. If AI gives a small group the ability to start a disaster that outpaces our defenses, the damage can become global. At the extreme, the concern is harm severe enough to threaten human survival.

There is also a separate danger: powerful autonomous AI pursuing goals that conflict with our survival and resisting efforts to stop it.

The existential risk depends on how far these capabilities advance and whether defenses keep pace. But we do not need certainty to reconsider the tradeoff. We are protecting people’s ability to resist tyranny while risking protection for someone’s ability to cause an irreversible catastrophe.

Other people cannot opt out of the consequences.

Privacy hides coordination. Permissionlessness removes the need for approval. Censorship resistance and unstoppability make intervention harder, even after a threat is clear.

These properties do not create destructive capabilities. They make their use harder to detect and stop. The same protections that frustrate a tyrant also frustrate anyone trying to prevent a disaster.

## How this could play out

Imagine four cases:

**An extremist group** wants to cause a pandemic but lacks the expertise. A sufficiently capable AI fills that gap. Private communication and payments help it coordinate and obtain resources in secret. Punishment offers little deterrence to people who want destruction and do not care about their own future.

**A company or billionaire** develops a powerful AI for profit. They receive much of the benefit while everyone shares the risk. They underestimate the danger or expect competitors to proceed anyway. Infrastructure beyond effective oversight makes safety restrictions easier to evade.

**A sincere researcher** believes their AI is safe and releases it through networks that resist suppression. If it is dangerous, copies already in circulation make the release hard to reverse. One person’s confidence exposes everyone to the consequences.

**An autonomous AI** resists shutdown. With access to funds, computing power, and services without ongoing human approval, it has a way to keep operating beyond its developer’s control.

Most people do not have to act recklessly for this to become dangerous. Repeated attempts and mistakes add to the risk. Goodwill alone does not solve that problem.

## Why cooperation gets harder

Even people who want to cooperate need to trust that others will keep their promises.

Imagine states or companies agreeing to restrict dangerous AI development. Hidden activity makes compliance harder to check and fuels pressure to race ahead. Activity that continues after discovery makes the rules harder to enforce.

Privacy does not always prevent verification. Some forms of compliance can be proved without exposing unrelated information. But when dangerous activity is both hidden and hard to stop, agreements lose credibility.

The infrastructure affects both the ability to cause harm and the ability to cooperate against it.

## The danger runs both ways

A neutral payment protocol cannot tell whether a transaction supports a dissident or a dangerous operation. Denying anyone the power to decide which uses are allowed also denies that power to a tyrant. The benefit and the danger are connected.

AI also gives authorities stronger tools for surveillance, manipulation, and repression. Removing private communication and independent payment systems leaves people with fewer ways to challenge those in control.

An authority enforcing global AI restrictions could entrench itself, suppress criticism, favor certain developers, or take catastrophic risks of its own. Pursuing safety does not prevent tyranny.

We need to trust both that others will follow safety rules and that those enforcing the rules will not abuse their power. More control can strengthen the first assurance while weakening the second.

Two losses may be irreversible:

- **Losing control:** once dangerous capabilities spread widely, stopping their use may be impossible.
- **Losing freedom:** once the infrastructure of resistance is gone, challenging those in power may be impossible.

Centralized control does not guarantee survival. Decentralized power does not guarantee protection from catastrophe.

I do not have a clean answer. People still need protection against concentrated power. I am less sure that making every activity unstoppable is a net good.

A tradeoff we accepted yesterday may become unacceptable tomorrow, even if the protocol stays exactly the same.

Cypherpunk values grew from taking the dangers of power seriously. Taking AI risk seriously belongs in that same tradition.
